01 · The problem

Two identical-looking records can end very differently.

A bag of codes lists what was recorded, not the order or the timing of care.

Here is why order matters. Take two applicants whose records look the same: chest pain and an electrocardiogram. Later, one receives an anxiety diagnosis and the other gets a troponin test. A bag of codes cannot tell them apart. That is a limit of the representation, not of the algorithm, so we keep the order of care and look for patterns in it. This is a schematic of two records described in our report, not a rate. Next, how we find the patterns.
02 · How we find them

Six steps take a guideline and raw records to checked care patterns.

{{cohort.n|int}} applicants, {{cohort.patterns}} patterns, {{cohort.sequences}} ordered sequences.

This is the whole method in six steps. First, a retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) language model reads the 2021 chest pain guideline of the American Heart Association and the American College of Cardiology and writes the code lists. Second, we take the {{cohort.n|int}} applicants with a chest pain code and pull their records from the Observational Medical Outcomes Partnership (OMOP) tables. Third, each applicant becomes a bag of codes. Fourth, non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) finds {{cohort.patterns}} care patterns, and we keep only those that survive re-runs. Fifth, we mine ordered sequences around chest pain and re-test them on unseen halves. Sixth, a language model searches PubMed for support; the literature can back an event but cannot test its order. Three data rules shape what you will see: visits are removed, refills are collapsed, and same-day steps follow a fixed order. Next, four terms.
02 · How we find them

Four terms carry the whole talk.

Each is defined once here and used the same way afterwards.

Four terms, defined once. A care pattern is a group of codes that tend to appear together in the same applicants' records. A sequence is a list of codes in the order they appear around chest pain; we keep the top {{cohort.perPattern}} per pattern. Times as common compares the share of a pattern's applicants who have a sequence with the share among everyone else. Validation re-tests a sequence on a random half it was not built from, {{cohort.heldRuns}} times. Each card ends with what the term does not mean. Next, the eight patterns.

We started with the question of whether anxiety or panic explains many chest pain cases. The data answer is awkward. {{psych.first|pct0}} of the {{psych.n|int}} applicants who have both a psychiatric code and a troponin test had the psychiatric code first. But no care pattern in any of our runs is led by anxiety or panic codes; those codes are spread thin across several patterns. So the signal exists in the timing but not as a pattern of its own. Psychiatric evidence here means anxiety, panic, or an evaluation or therapy code. Next, how well the sequences hold up.
03 · What we found

{{verdicts.hold}} of {{verdicts.total}} sequences hold in validation; {{verdicts.unstable}} do not.

The top {{cohort.perPattern}} sequences of each pattern, colored by verdict, with times as common in each cell.

Every pattern has three top sequences, so there are {{verdicts.total}} cells. We re-tested each on random halves of the pattern. {{verdicts.solid}} are solid: they hold, and the order shows in the dates. {{verdicts.suggestive}} are suggestive: they hold, but the steps happen on the same day, so order is not evidence. {{verdicts.unstable}} are unstable and should not be used. Check-up and women's health care carry most of the unstable ones. Repeatable does not mean clinically true. Next, four limits of what a code can tell us.
04 · What codes cannot tell us

Four limits of what a code can tell us.

They come from how claims are recorded, and they bound every pattern shown.

Four limits to weigh as you read everything else. First, a code shows that a test happened, not what it found: we cannot see a troponin level or an electrocardiogram reading. Second, many codes are generic: a blood draw or a general examination appears at most check-ups, and we cannot see which troponin test was used. Third, claims carry dates, not times, so {{limits.sameDay}} of {{limits.total}} sequences are mostly same-day and their order is a rule. Fourth, we hold no confirmed cardiac outcome, so nothing is validated. Next, how a pattern becomes a rule.
05 · From pattern to rule

Each pattern becomes one yes-or-no rule built from three parts.

Example: the emergency work-up. The window and the counts are drafts with no calibration yet.

To be usable, a pattern has to become a rule. Each rule is a yes-or-no question with three parts: chest pain is present, at least {{rule.minEntities}} of the pattern's main events fall within {{rule.windowDays}} day of chest pain, and at least one of {{rule.sequences}} ordered sequences is present. For the emergency work-up the main events are troponin, intravenous push, lipase and chest X-ray. The window and the count are drafts. We have not calibrated them, and for several patterns the main events sit months before chest pain, so the window may not fit. That is one of your questions. Next, where underwriting sign-off comes in.
06 · Where underwriting sign-off comes in?

Is it clinically real? Would it change what an underwriter looks at?

This is where we would like your help. For each pattern, two questions. Is it clinically real, or only a database correlation? And would it change what an underwriter looks at? We have left the grid empty on purpose, because placing the patterns ourselves would be our opinion and not your judgment. We can do it live, one pattern at a time, or you can take the page away. The backup pages in the appendix show each pattern's events and sequences. Next, five specific questions.
06 · Where underwriting sign-off comes in?

Five questions we need answered.

Each one maps to a slide. Your answers decide what stays in the rules.

Five questions. One: which patterns describe a real cardiac work-up, and which are routine care that only sits next to chest pain. Two: does the order of steps matter clinically, or is it a same-day artifact. Three: which single events carry weight on their own, and which are generic. Four: what is missing from a code that you would need, a result, a test type, a severity. Five: are the draft settings sensible, a one-day window and three or more events. We will stay on whichever you choose. Thank you.
