Acute chest pain is a symptom: a currently present or recently reported, new-onset sensation of pain felt anywhere in the chest region, including retrosternal, precordial, lateral, pleuritic and chest-wall locations, on the left side, the right side or both. It is defined by the pain the patient feels and not by its cause or diagnosis, so it includes pain arising from cardiac, pulmonary, gastrointestinal, musculoskeletal, psychogenic or any other cause, and pain whose cause is not established; no specific pathological process or tissue abnormality is required. It excludes chronic or long-standing chest pain, pain that has fully resolved and is history only, suspected or ruled-out pain that the patient does not feel, pain felt only at other sites such as the arm, jaw, back or upper abdomen without chest pain, and chest pain related to pregnancy, including the postpartum period. The concept is the symptom alone, so the underlying disorders that can cause it, such as myocardial infarction, angina, pulmonary embolism, pneumonia, reflux disease or costochondritis, are separate concepts, as are abnormal test findings, procedures and drugs. The definition deliberately leaves unconstrained all severities from mild to severe, all ages and life stages, all clinically allowable causes, all sides and sublocations within the chest, and any fixed time limit for "acute" beyond new or recent onset.
