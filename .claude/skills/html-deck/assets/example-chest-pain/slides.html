Here is the goal, up front: we need you to tell us what each predicate and composite still needs, and which are clinically real. A predicate is a yes-or-no question about an applicant's records, and a composite combines predicates into a care pattern. Nothing in this deck recommends an underwriting decision. Now the problem we started from. Two of the five chest pain codes, other chest pain and chest pain unspecified, carry {{icd.top2|pct0}} of applicants, and both are nonspecific. Chest pain can be cardiac, musculoskeletal, digestive or anxiety-related, and the code does not say which. This is the entry rule of every pattern: it records no type of pain, not exertional, not at rest, not atypical. The 2021 guideline advises against the word atypical, so we say chest pain. 02 · How we find them

Six steps take a guideline and raw records to checked care patterns.

{{cohort.n|int}} applicants, {{cohort.patterns}} patterns, {{cohort.sequences}} ordered sequences.

This is the whole method in six steps. First, a retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) language model reads the 2021 chest pain guideline of the American Heart Association and the American College of Cardiology and writes the code lists. Second, we take the {{cohort.n|int}} applicants with a chest pain code and pull their records from the Observational Medical Outcomes Partnership (OMOP) tables. Third, each applicant becomes a bag of codes. Fourth, non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) finds {{cohort.patterns}} care patterns, and we keep only those that survive re-runs. Fifth, we mine ordered sequences around chest pain and re-test them on unseen halves. Sixth, a language model searches PubMed for support; the literature can back an event but cannot test its order. Three data rules shape what you will see: visits are removed, refills are collapsed, and same-day steps follow a fixed order. Next, four terms.
02 · How we find them

Four terms carry the whole talk.

Each is defined once here and used the same way afterwards.

Four terms, defined once. A care pattern is a group of standardized codes (SNOMED) that tend to appear together in the same applicants' records. A sequence is a list of SNOMED codes in the order they appear around chest pain; we keep the top {{cohort.perPattern}} per pattern. Times as common compares the share of a pattern's applicants who have a sequence with the share among everyone else. Validation re-tests a sequence on a random half it was not built from, {{cohort.heldRuns}} times. Each card ends with what the term does not mean. Next, what a predicate is.
03 · The predicates

A predicate says a test happened, not what it found.

A predicate is one yes-or-no question about an applicant's records. Troponin is the example.

For the roomWhich missing detail would change how you read a predicate: the result, the test type, or a severity?

A predicate is the smallest building block. It asks one yes-or-no question of an applicant's records. Take troponin. The code list holds {{preds.examples.troponin}} codes, and any one counts. The event has to fall within {{rule.windowDays|word}} day of a chest pain event. The answer is true or false, nothing more. It does not say what the test found: the troponin level, whether the test was high-sensitivity or standard, and the reading of an electrocardiogram are not in the data. So a true predicate means care was delivered, not that anything was abnormal. Next, the whole set of predicates.

05 · From pattern to rule

Each pattern becomes one yes-or-no rule built from three parts.

Example: the emergency work-up. The window and the counts are drafts with no calibration yet.

To be usable, a pattern has to become a rule, which our page calls a composite. Each rule is a yes-or-no question with three parts: chest pain is present, at least {{rule.minEntities}} of the pattern's main events fall within {{rule.windowDays}} day of chest pain, and at least one of {{rule.sequences}} ordered sequences is present. For the emergency work-up the main events are troponin, intravenous push, lipase and chest X-ray. The window and the count are drafts. We have not calibrated them, and for several patterns the main events sit months before chest pain, so the window may not fit. That is one of your questions. Appendix · Identical records

Two identical-looking records can end very differently.

A bag of codes lists what was recorded, not the order or the timing of care.

Backup. Here is why order matters. Take two applicants whose records look the same: chest pain and an electrocardiogram. Later, one receives an anxiety diagnosis and the other gets a troponin test. A bag of codes cannot tell them apart. That is a limit of the representation, not of the algorithm, so we keep the order of care and look for patterns in it. This is a schematic of two records described in our report, not a rate.
Appendix · Limitations

Limitations

Four limits of what a code can tell us.

Backup. Four limits to weigh as you read everything else. First, a code shows that a test happened, not what it found: we cannot see a troponin level or an electrocardiogram reading. Second, many codes are generic: a blood draw or a general examination appears at most check-ups, and we cannot see which troponin test was used. Third, claims carry dates, not times, so {{limits.sameDay}} of {{limits.total}} sequences are mostly same-day and their order is a rule. Fourth, we hold no confirmed cardiac outcome, so nothing is validated.
